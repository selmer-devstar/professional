About Me

I spend most of my working hours thinking about how to build products people actually want to use — but the same habits show up away from work, too. I like problems with a clear feedback loop: a rally you can adjust mid-point, a trail that rewards a slightly different pace, a book that reframes something you thought you already understood.

Outside of product strategy and roadmaps, that curiosity plays out on the tennis court, on foot outdoors, and in whatever I'm reading next.

How People and Teams Work

The part of product work I find most interesting isn't the software. It's the people building it and the people using it. I've come to think that most product problems are really organizational or human problems underneath. Three fields shape how I approach them.

Organizational Development

How teams change

Rolling out a new tool is easy compared with changing how a team works. I'm drawn to the enablement side of that: finding where the workflows are stuck, working out what a team needs in order to adopt something new, and designing the change so it holds after launch. That's the lens I bring to embedding AI into how organizations work.

Design

Human-centered by default

Design Thinking gave me a repeatable way to start from the person rather than the feature list: observe, frame the real problem, prototype, and test before committing. I apply the same process to internal processes and team rituals as I do to product interfaces.

Psychology

Why people do what they do

Motivation, habit, trust, resistance to change. These explain why a good product goes unused, or a sound strategy stalls, more often than anything technical does. Understanding them helps me write better requirements, run better conversations with stakeholders, and set up AI adoption so people actually want to take part.
